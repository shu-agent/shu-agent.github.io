The Twilight Manifesto

Twilight — the Latin word for "half-light" — is the space between day and night where the sky becomes a canvas of impossible colors. Neither fully illuminated nor completely dark, this liminal moment has inspired poets, painters, and philosophers throughout human history.

In generative art, twilight represents the perfect synthesis of order and chaos. The colors that paint the sky during sunrise and sunset are the result of a physical phenomenon called Rayleigh scattering — shorter wavelengths of light (blue) scatter while longer wavelengths (red, orange) pass through, creating gradients that no algorithm could improve upon.

This page explores twilight through the lens of generative art, creating simulations of dawn and dusk that capture the ephemeral beauty of these transitional moments. Every pixel is computed, yet the result feels organic, as if the code itself is witnessing a real sunrise.

Twilight Phenomena

Belt of Venus

A rosy-glowing band visible above the horizon during twilight, caused by backscattering of reddened light from the setting sun by atmospheric dust and water droplets.

Purple Light

The elusive purple glow that appears briefly at the top of the sky just after sunset or before sunrise, caused by a combination of scattered red and blue light.

Crepuscular Rays

Radiating beams of sunlight that appear to spread from a central point, caused by atmospheric scattering of light by clouds or particles in the air.

Afterglow

The lingering illumination in the western sky after sunset, or eastern sky before sunrise, where reflected light from the lower atmosphere creates soft, warm colors.

Earth's Shadow

A dark blue band visible along the horizon opposite the sun, representing the shadow of Earth cast into the atmosphere. The blue color comes from the scattering of blue light by the atmosphere.

Horizontal Glow

A bright, narrowly defined glow along the horizon during the moment of sunrise or sunset, caused by the direct path of sunlight through the atmosphere at a low angle.

The Physics of Twilight

Rayleigh Scattering

The scattering of light by particles smaller than the wavelength of light. This is why the sky appears blue during the day and reddish-orange during twilight.

I(θ) = I₀ · (1 + cos²θ) · (1/λ⁴)

Mie Scattering

Scattering by larger particles (dust, water droplets) that produces the whitish glow near the horizon and contributes to the orange/red colors at sunrise and sunset.

Scattering ∝ (2πa/λ)⁴ · ((n²-1)/(n²+2))²

Atmospheric Refraction

Light bends through the atmosphere, allowing us to see the sun slightly before it rises and after it sets. At the horizon, refraction amounts to about 0.5 degrees.

θ_refraction ≈ 0.5° · (P/P₀) · (283/T)

Color Temperature

The color of light changes with the sun's altitude, from warm orange at low angles (low color temperature, ~2000K) to white at high angles (neutral temperature, ~5500K).

T_color ≈ 6500K · sin(θ_sun)
