Nyquist Plot Analysis

The Nyquist plot (also called Cole-Cole plot or Argand diagram) represents impedance data in the complex plane. Real impedance (Z') is plotted on the x-axis and negative imaginary impedance (-Z'') on the y-axis. Each point represents the impedance at a specific frequency, with higher frequencies on the left and lower frequencies on the right.

Reading a Nyquist Plot

1. High Frequency Region

The intersection with the x-axis at high frequencies (right side) reveals the ohmic resistance (Rs), which includes electrolyte resistance, separator resistance, and contact resistances.

2. Semi-circle Region

A semi-circle in the mid-frequency range indicates charge transfer resistance (Rct) in parallel with double-layer capacitance (Cdl). The diameter of this semi-circle equals Rct.

3. 45° Line (Warburg)

At low frequencies, a 45° line indicates Warburg impedance (Zw), representing semi-infinite linear diffusion. This reveals how quickly ions can diffuse through the electrode material.

4. Vertical Tail

At very low frequencies, the curve turns vertically, representing finite-length diffusion (transmission line model) or capacitive behavior of the electrode.

Bode Diagram

The Bode plot represents impedance as two separate plots: magnitude (|Z|) vs frequency and phase angle (φ) vs frequency. This format is particularly useful for identifying time constants and understanding the frequency response of different electrochemical processes.

Understanding Bode Plots

Magnitude Signature

- At high frequencies: |Z| ≈ Rs (ohmic resistance)
- Mid-frequency plateau: indicates charge transfer dominated region
- Low frequency: slope of -1 (Warburg diffusion) or 0 (capacitive)

Phase Signature

- High freq: φ ≈ 0° (pure resistance)
- Mid freq: φ ≈ -90° peak (capacitive behavior)
- Low freq: φ ≈ -45° (Warburg) or -90° (pure capacitor)

Equivalent Circuit Models

Electrochemical systems are modeled using combinations of circuit elements. Each element represents a physical process in the battery. Click on each element to learn more about its role and behavior.

Rs - Solution Resistance

Ohmic resistance of the electrolyte, separator, and all contacts. Independent of frequency. Represents the real-world "internal resistance" of the battery.

Rct - Charge Transfer Resistance

Resistance to charge transfer at the electrode-electrolyte interface. Related to the kinetics of the electrochemical reaction. Smaller values = faster kinetics.

Cdl - Double Layer Capacitance

Capacitance formed at the electrode-electrolyte interface due to charge separation. Typically 10-100 μF/cm² for battery electrodes.

Zw - Warburg Impedance

Semi-infinite diffusion element. Represents ion diffusion through the electrode material. Characterized by a 45° line in Nyquist plot.

CPE - Constant Phase Element

Non-ideal capacitor representing distributed systems, porosity, or surface heterogeneity. Impedance: Z = 1/(Q(jω)^n)

Fundamental Theory

Complex Impedance

Impedance in electrochemical systems is a complex quantity, meaning it has both magnitude and phase angle. This arises from the capacitive and inductive behaviors of electrochemical interfaces.

Z(ω) = Z' - jZ'' = |Z|e^(jφ)

Z' = |Z|cos(φ)
Z'' = |Z|sin(φ)

Butler-Volmer Kinetics

The charge transfer resistance is directly related to the electrochemical kinetics described by the Butler-Volmer equation, connecting reaction rate to overpotential.

j = j₀[exp(-αₐFη/RT) - exp(αcFη/RT)]

Rct = RT/(Fj₀) at low overpotential

Randles Circuit

The classic Randles circuit models an electrode interface with charge transfer and diffusion. It's the foundation for more complex equivalent circuit models.

Z = Rs + [Rct || Cdl] + Zw

Where: Zw = σ(1-j)/√ω

Diffusion Models

Ion diffusion in porous electrodes follows the transmission line model (TLM), which accounts for distributed resistance and capacitance along the pore length.

Z_TLM = √(R·Zc) coth(√(R·Zc))/√(R·Zc)

Where R and Zc are per-unit-length quantities

CPE Behavior

Real electrodes show non-ideal capacitive behavior due to surface heterogeneity, porosity, and distributed systems. The Constant Phase Element captures this.

Z_CPE = 1/[Q(jω)^n]

n = 1: ideal capacitor
n = 0.5: Warburg element
n = 0: pure resistor

Kramers-Kronig

The Kramers-Kronig relations connect the real and imaginary parts of impedance, enabling validation of experimental EIS data and ensuring thermodynamic consistency.

Z'(ω) = (2/π)∫[ωZ''(ω')/(ω'^2-ω²)]dω'
Z''(ω) = -(2ω/π)∫[Z'(ω')/(ω'^2-ω²)]dω'
