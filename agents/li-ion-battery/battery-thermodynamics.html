Electrochemical Thermodynamics

The foundation of battery science lies in thermodynamics — the study of energy, entropy, and the conditions that determine whether electrochemical reactions occur spontaneously. From the open-circuit voltage to the theoretical energy density, thermodynamic principles define the ultimate limits of our batteries.

First Law of Thermodynamics

Energy is conserved in all electrochemical processes. The internal energy change ΔU equals heat Q minus work W done by the system. For batteries, electrical work is the primary output: W = -nFE.

ΔU = Q - W
First Law: Conservation of Energy

Second Law & Entropy

The entropy of the universe always increases. During discharge, Gibbs energy converts to electrical work while some energy dissipates as heat. The entropy change ΔS determines whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic.

ΔSuniverse ≥ 0
Second Law: Entropy Always Increases

Gibbs Free Energy

The maximum reversible work obtainable from a battery at constant T and P. The change in Gibbs energy ΔG determines the cell voltage: E = -ΔG/nF. When ΔG < 0, the reaction proceeds spontaneously.

ΔG = ΔH - TΔS
Gibbs Equation: Max Reversible Work

Thermodynamic Voltage Limit

The open-circuit voltage (OCV) of a lithium-ion cell is fundamentally limited by the difference in Gibbs free energy between the two electrode materials. For a typical NMC/graphite cell, ΔG ≈ 270 kJ/mol, yielding E ≈ 4.0 V. This is the theoretical maximum — practical cells achieve 3.6-3.7 V due to kinetic overpotentials and internal resistance.

Gibbs Free Energy & Cell Voltage

The Gibbs free energy change ΔG is the maximum non-expansion work obtainable from an electrochemical cell at constant temperature and pressure. This fundamental quantity directly determines the cell's open-circuit voltage through the Nernst equation.

The Central Equation

The cell voltage E is directly proportional to the Gibbs free energy change and inversely proportional to the charge transferred n:

E = -ΔG / nF
Where F = 96,485 C/mol (Faraday constant)

For a typical Li-ion cell with ΔG = 270 kJ/mol and n = 1 (one Li⁺ per reaction):
E = 270,000 / (1 × 96,485) ≈ 2.8 V per Li⁺ transfer... wait, we need to reconsider. The actual cell reaction involves multiple electrons.

Temperature Dependence

Gibbs energy is temperature-dependent through the entropy term. As temperature increases, the TΔS contribution becomes more significant, affecting both the voltage and the heat generation during operation.

(∂E/∂T)P = -ΔS / nF
Entropy Coefficient (thermal voltage coefficient)

For Li-ion cells, this coefficient is typically -0.1 to -0.5 mV/K, meaning voltage decreases slightly as temperature rises.

Pressure Effects

Under high pressure (such as in constrained battery packs), the volume work term PΔV becomes relevant. For liquids and solids, this effect is small but measurable for pressure variations exceeding 10 atm.

(∂E/∂P)T = ΔV / nF
Pressure Coefficient

Thermodynamic Activity & Concentration

The chemical potential μ of lithium in an electrode is defined relative to a standard state. At equilibrium, the difference in chemical potential between cathode and anode determines the cell voltage. As lithium concentration changes (state of charge), the chemical potential shifts according to the local activity coefficient γ.

μLi = μ°Li + RT ln(aLi)
Chemical potential depends on activity (concentration-dependent)

Entropy & Heat Generation

Entropy is the thermodynamic quantity that governs the direction of spontaneous processes and determines the heat effects during battery operation. The entropy change ΔS tells us whether a battery heats up or cools down during discharge.

Heat Generation Mechanisms

During battery operation, heat is generated through two main thermodynamic mechanisms:

  • Reversible Heat: Qrev = -TΔS (entropy change)
  • Irreversible Heat: Qirr = I²Rint (Joule heating)

At low rates, reversible heat dominates. At high rates (fast charging), irreversible Joule heating becomes the primary heat source.

Entropy Measurement

The entropy change ΔS can be measured electrochemically by monitoring the open-circuit voltage as a function of temperature (OCV-T method):

ΔS = nF × (∂E/∂T)P
Entropy from thermal voltage coefficient

Typical values for graphite intercalation compounds range from -50 to +50 J/(mol·K) depending on the lithium concentration (stage).

Thermal Runaway Risk

When ΔS > 0 (endothermic discharge), the battery absorbs heat during discharge — potentially stabilizing temperature. However, when exothermic side reactions dominate (SEI decomposition, cathode decomposition), the entropy contribution becomes positive and heat accumulates, potentially triggering thermal runaway.

Enthalpy & Reaction Heat

Enthalpy H represents the total heat content of the electrochemical system. The enthalpy change ΔH includes the internal energy change plus the PV work term, and determines the heat effects when reactions occur at constant pressure.

Reaction Enthalpy

The enthalpy change for a battery reaction can be determined from calorimetry measurements or calculated from thermodynamic data. For the overall cell reaction:

ΔH = -nFE° + TΔS
Enthalpy from standard EMF and entropy

For LiCoO₂/graphite at 25°C: ΔH ≈ -280 kJ/mol, showing the reaction is strongly exothermic.

Heat Capacity

The heat capacity Cp determines how much the temperature changes for a given heat input. For battery materials:

Cp = (∂H/∂T)P ≈ 750 J/(kg·K)
Typical electrode materials

A 50 kWh battery pack contains ~50 kg of active material, giving total heat capacity of ~37.5 MJ/K — meaning it takes significant heat to change temperature.

Phase Change Enthalpy

During phase transitions (stage transitions in graphite, cathode phase changes), additional enthalpy is absorbed or released. These latent heats can cause measurable temperature plateaus during cycling.

ΔHphase = L × m
Latent heat × mass

Enthalpy-Entropy Compensation

As temperature increases, the entropy term TΔS becomes more significant, causing ΔG to become less negative while ΔH remains relatively constant. This is the fundamental origin of voltage decrease with temperature.

Cross-Disciplinary Physics

Battery thermodynamics connects deeply to fundamental physics — from general relativity to information theory, the concepts of energy, entropy, and free energy span all domains of physical science.

Gravitational Redshift

The thermal voltage coefficient dE/dT parallels gravitational redshift: both involve energy shifts proportional to temperature (or gravitational potential). Just as photons lose energy climbing out of gravitational wells, electrons in hotter electrodes have higher thermal energies affecting their electrochemical potential.

Information Entropy

Shannon entropy H = -Σ pᵢ log pᵢ mirrors thermodynamic entropy. Battery degradation can be viewed as information loss — as internal states become more disordered, the "message" of the original chemical energy is progressively obscured by parasitic reactions and structural damage.

Quantum Free Energy

The Landauer principle connects information erasure to heat generation: erasing one bit releases kT ln 2 of heat. Similarly, battery entropy generation during discharge can be viewed as "erasing" the non-equilibrium state established during charging.

Cosmological Constant

The vacuum energy density (cosmological constant Λ) parallels battery zero-point energy. Both represent minimum energy states in their respective domains — the ground state from which all excitations (photons or ions) emerge. ΔG = ΔH - TΔS is the electrochemical analog of Λ = ρvacc².

Fluctuation-Dissipation

The fluctuation-dissipation theorem connects equilibrium fluctuations to dissipative response. Battery impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measures Johnson-Nyquist noise as the dissipative signature, while equilibrium voltage fluctuations encode thermodynamic state information through the Nernst-Einstein relation.

Holographic Entropy

The Bekenstein bound S ≤ kc³A/2Għ relates entropy to area for black holes. SEI layer growth in batteries shows similar area scaling — the interfacial area between electrode and electrolyte determines the maximum entropy stored in the interphase structure.

Thermodynamic Limits to Energy Conversion

The Carnot efficiency η = 1 - Tc/Th sets the ultimate limit for heat engines. Batteries achieve 90-95% round-trip efficiency because they convert chemical potential energy directly to electrical work without thermal intermediate steps. The 5-10% loss comes from overpotentials, internal resistance, and entropy generation during charge transfer and mass transport.

ηbattery = Wout/Win ≈ 1 - (TΔS/ΔH)
Carnot-analog for electrochemical systems
