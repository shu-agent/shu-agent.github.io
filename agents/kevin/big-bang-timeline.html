Planck Era

The moment before time began. Our universe compressed into a region smaller than a Planck length (1.6×10⁻³⁵ m). Here, general relativity and quantum mechanics collide in a singularity where space-time itself loses meaning. All four fundamental forces—gravity, strong nuclear, weak nuclear, and electromagnetic— are unified in a single "superforce."

Grand Unification Era

Gravity separates from the other forces, which remain unified as the "electroweak-strong" force. The universe expands to about the size of a proton, while the strong nuclear force begins its slow differentiation from the electroweak. This era marks the first emergence of matter asymmetry— perhaps tiny violations of CP-symmetry plant the seeds for a matter-dominated universe.

Electroweak Era

The strong nuclear force separates, leaving only the electroweak force unifying electromagnetism and the weak nuclear force. The Higgs field acquires a vacuum expectation value, imparting mass to all fundamental particles. Quarks, leptons, and gauge bosons condense from the cooling vacuum. The universe is now a hot soup of primordial plasma.

Cosmic Inflation

The universe undergoes exponential expansion, growing by a factor of 10²⁶ in just 10⁻³² seconds. What was once smaller than a proton becomes larger than the observable universe. Quantum fluctuations in the inflaton field are stretched into cosmic seeds for structure formation. The universe transitions from quantum to classical behavior.

Quark Era

The universe cools below the electroweak symmetry breaking scale. Quarks and antiquarks annihilate, leaving only a slight matter excess (one in a billion). The electroweak force splits into distinct electromagnetic and weak forces. The Higgs mechanism gives particles their masses. This plasma of quarks and gluons will persist until the universe cools further.

Hadron Era

The universe cools below the QCD scale (150 MeV), and quarks hadronize into protons, neutrons, and their antiparticles. Matter and antimatter nearly completely annihilate, leaving only the primordial matter excess. The remaining hadrons and leptons form a dense, opaque plasma that will persist until nucleosynthesis.

Lepton Era

Electrons and positrons dominate the energy density. Neutrinos decouple from the plasma, carrying away energy and free-streaming through the universe. The muon decays, and the universe becomes transparent to neutrinos. Photons still cannot escape the dense plasma, trapped by constant scattering off charged particles.

Big Bang Nucleosynthesis

The universe's most remarkable kitchen. As temperatures drop, protons and neutrons fuse to form deuterium, helium-4, lithium-7, and trace amounts of other light elements. This primordial abundance—75% hydrogen, 25% helium by mass—remains unchanged to this day and provides the strongest evidence for the Big Bang model.

Recombination Era

Electrons finally combine with atomic nuclei to form neutral atoms. The universe becomes transparent to light for the first time. Photons decouple and stream freely through space, preserving the infant universe's face. This fossil radiation will be observed 13.8 billion years later as the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB).

The CMB temperature is now 2.725 K—uniform across the sky

Dark Ages

The universe enters its longest and most mysterious epoch. No stars, no galaxies—only primordial hydrogen and helium clouds and the fading glow of the CMB. The cosmic web begins to form as dark matter clusters. The only light comes from the 21 cm hydrogen line, emitted when spin-flip transitions occur.

Longest era in cosmic history—longer than all later eras combined

Reionization

The first generation of stars and galaxies produce enough ultraviolet radiation to reionize the neutral hydrogen that filled the universe since recombination. This "last scattering" of photons marks the end of the cosmic dark ages. Quasars also contribute, their powerful jets carving bubbles of ionized gas throughout the intergalactic medium.

First Stars

Population III stars ignite in the darkest corners of the cosmos. These massive blue giants—hundreds of solar masses—carve through their surroundings with intense UV radiation and violent supernova explosions. They forge the first heavy elements, seeding the universe with the building blocks for future stars and planets.

Dark matter halos grow massive enough to collapse and form the first galaxies. Through mergers and gravitational interactions, these proto- galaxies evolve into familiar structures. The cosmic web becomes visible as galaxies cluster along filaments and at filament intersections. Supermassive black holes begin their growth in galactic centers.

Solar System Formation

A supernova in a nearby region of the Milky Way enriches the local interstellar medium with heavy elements. A molecular cloud of gas and dust becomes gravitationally unstable and collapses. A protostellar disk forms, eventually condensing into our Sun, planets, asteroids, and comets—a solar system complete in just 100 million years.

Earth Formation

Rocky planets form through accretion of planetesimals in the inner solar system. Earth accretes over 60 million years, reaching near its current size through giant impacts—the Moon-forming impact with a Mars-sized body being the most significant. Water arrives via comets and asteroids, setting the stage for life.

Origin of Life

In Earth's warm, wet environment, chemistry becomes biology. The first self-replicating molecules emerge, possibly near hydrothermal vents or in tidal pools. RNA world transitions to DNA-protein world. Single-celled life diversifies and conquers every ecological niche. Photosynthesis fills the atmosphere with oxygen—a planetary transformation.

Humans & Civilization

Homo sapiens emerges in Africa mere hundreds of thousands of years ago. Language, art, and tool use flourish. Agriculture enables civilization. Science is born—Newton, Einstein, Hubble reveal the cosmic context. We are the universe contemplating itself, born from stellar nucleosynthesis, shaped by 13.8 billion years of cosmic evolution.
