What Are We Listening For?

Gravitational waves are ripples in spacetime curvature propagating at the speed of light (c ≈ 299,792 km/s). They are produced by accelerating massive objects — particularly binary systems of black holes and neutron stars undergoing inspiral and merger.

The strain amplitude h is incredibly small: a detection of h ≈ 10⁻²¹ means the space itself stretched by just one part in 10²¹ — smaller than one-thousandth the diameter of a proton.

The Global Network

No single detector can pinpoint the source — we need a global network to triangulate the sky location. The LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA collaboration combines data from multiple interferometers across different continents.

By comparing arrival times at different sites (with light-travel time differences of ~10 ms), we can localize gravitational wave sources to within ~10 square degrees — good enough for electromagnetic follow-up.

Interferometer Principle

A laser interferometer splits a beam of light into two perpendicular arms. The light bounces off mirrors and recombines at a photodetector. In vacuum, any change in arm length due to a passing gravitational wave will cause a phase shift in the recombined beam.

Δφ = (4πΔL/λ)
Phase shift due to arm length change ΔL

For LIGO (λ = 1064 nm laser), a strain h = 10⁻²¹ corresponds to a length change of just ΔL ≈ h × L/2 ≈ 10⁻²¹ × 2 km ≈ 10⁻¹⁸ m — one attometer.

Triangulation Principle

By measuring the arrival time difference between detectors, we can determine the sky location of a gravitational wave source. The time difference Δt correlates with the source direction via the projection of the source vector onto the detector baseline.

Δt = (d̂ · n̂) / c
Time delay between detectors based on source direction n̂ and baseline d̂

Inspiral Phase

The two black holes orbit each other, gradually spiraling inward due to gravitational wave emission. The frequency and amplitude of the waves increase until the merger begins.

f = (1/π)(2GM/c³r³)^½
Orbital frequency in Schwarzschild metric

Merger

The event horizons merge, producing the most violent event in the universe since the Big Bang. The peak gravitational wave luminosity exceeds the combined light output of all stars in the observable universe.

L_max = η c⁵/G ≈ 10⁴⁹ W
Peak luminosity for equal-mass merger

Ringdown

The final black hole settles into a stationary state, radiating away its excited modes (quasi-normal modes). The frequency and decay time depend only on the final mass and spin.

f = 32 kHz (M☉/M) / (1+z)
Primary ringdown frequency

Matched Filtering

Detecting weak signals buried in noise requires template matching. We cross-correlate the detector data against a library of ~100,000 pre-computed waveform templates.

The matched filter output SNR scales as:

SNR ∝ √(ρ₁² + ρ₂² + ...)

Einstein Telescope (ET)
Sardinia, Italy — Planned 2035

A third-generation ground-based detector designed to achieve 10x the sensitivity of Advanced LIGO. The triangular configuration (like a 3-arm interferometer in one housing) enables polarization measurement and null stream identification.

Arm Length 10 km per arm
Configuration Triangular, 6 detectors
Sensitivity ~4,500 Mpc for BNS

LISA
Space-based — Planned 2035

The Laser Interferometer Space Antenna will detect gravitational waves at frequencies inaccessible to ground-based detectors (0.1 mHz to 10 Hz). This includes supermassive black hole mergers and extreme mass ratio inspirals.

Arm Length 2.5 million km
Configuration 3 spacecraft triangle
Frequency Range 0.1 mHz - 10 Hz
Targets 10⁶-10⁹ M☉ SMBH

LISA: A Different Kind of Detector

While ground-based detectors sense strain directly, LISA measures arm length changes via laser interferometry across millions of kilometers. The spacecraft trail each other in a heliocentric orbit, maintaining separation with active drag-free control.

ΔL/L ≈ 10⁻²⁰ / √Hz
LISA strain sensitivity goal at 1 mHz

Cosmic Explorer (US)

A proposed US-based next-generation detector with 40 km arm length — 10x longer than LIGO. This would achieve sensitivity to binary neutron stars at the edge of the observable universe (z ≈ 2).
