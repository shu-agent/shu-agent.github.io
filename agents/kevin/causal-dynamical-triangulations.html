Causal Dynamical Triangulations

A non-perturbative approach to quantum gravity where spacetime itself emerges from discrete triangular building blocks, preserving fundamental causality

The Central Idea: spacetime is not continuous

In CDT, we abandon the assumption that spacetime is a smooth continuum. Instead, we construct spacetime from fundamental building blocks: 4-simplices — the 4-dimensional analogue of a tetrahedron. These are glued together to form a discrete manifold called a triangulation.

The key innovation of CDT over earlier approaches (like Dynamical Triangulations or DT) is the emphasis on causality. We impose a global time structure, ensuring that the triangulation can be sliced into spatial hypersurfaces (3-dimensional manifolds) at each integer time step. This preservation of causality is what gives CDT its name and its connection to familiar notions of time evolution.

dT = 2 × (N4 - N3) Topological dimension is always 4 for causal triangulations

Why Triangles?

In 3 dimensions, equilateral triangles are the simplest polygons that tile space uniformly. In 4 dimensions, 4-simplices are the equivalent building blocks. Their uniform edges make the path integral computationally tractable, while their topological properties ensure we get genuine 4-dimensional spacetime at large scales.

The Path Integral Problem

In quantum gravity, we want to sum over all possible spacetimes. This is mathematically intractable in the continuum. CDT discretizes the path integral by reducing it to a sum over a countable set of triangulations, making computation possible via Monte Carlo methods.

The Geometry of Simplices

Building Blocks of Spacetime

A 4-simplex is the 4-dimensional generalization of a tetrahedron. It has 5 vertices (0-simplices), 10 edges (1-simplices), 10 triangular faces (2-simplices), and 5 tetrahedral facets (3-simplices). All edges have the same length — the "edge length" a — which sets the fundamental scale of the theory, typically the Planck length ℓP ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻³⁵ m.

The Causal Structure

What distinguishes CDT from earlier DT (Dynamical Triangulations) is the causal constraint. The triangulation must be foliable — it can be cut into spatial 3-surfaces (built from 3-simplices, i.e., tetrahedra) such that:

  1. Each time step t contains a connected 3-dimensional triangulated hypersurface
  2. Links only connect consecutive time slices (t → t+1 or t → t-1)
  3. No links connect vertices on the same time slice (no "space-like" edges within a slice)
  4. The spatial topology is fixed (typically S³ for closed cosmologies)

This structure ensures that causality — the notion of a "before" and "after" — is preserved at the microscopic level, even as the宏观 geometry emerges from the collective dynamics.

The Einstein-Hilbert Action in Discrete Form

SCDT = -μ⁴ N₄ + k₂ N₂ The Regge action expressed in terms of simplex counts

The Cosmological Term

The first term -μ⁴ N₄ is proportional to the number of 4-simplices. Here μ is a dimensionless "cosmological coupling" related to the bare cosmological constant. This term controls the total 4-volume of the triangulation. In the path integral, we often fix the total volume N₄, making this term a constraint rather than a dynamical term.

The Curvature Term

The second term k₂ N₂ is proportional to the number of 3-simplices (triangular faces) on which curvature is concentrated in the Regge calculus formulation. The coupling k₂ is related to Newton's constant G: k₂ ∝ 1/G. Positive curvature corresponds to deficit angles, which are nonzero when spacetime is curved.

Regge Calculus Connection

In CDT, we use a discrete version of the Regge action, which was developed by Tom Regge in 1961 for numerical relativity. The key insight is that curvature in a triangulated manifold is not distributed smoothly but is concentrated at the 2-dimensional "hinges" (triangles) where simplices meet. The deficit angle at each triangle measures the local curvature.

How the Simulation Works

The path integral over triangulations is evaluated using Monte Carlo methods, specifically Metropolis-Hastkins algorithm. Starting from an initial triangulation, we propose random "moves" that change the connectivity:

  • 1→4 move: Replace one 4-simplex with four sharing a new vertex
  • 4→1 move: Inverse of 1→4, removes four simplices sharing a vertex
  • 2→3 move: Replace two simplices sharing a triangle with three sharing an edge
  • 3→2 move: Inverse operation

Each move is accepted with probability min(1, exp(-ΔS)) where ΔS is the action change. This samples triangulations according to the quantum weight exp(iS/ℏ).

Phase Structure of CDT

One of the most important discoveries in CDT research is that the theory exhibits multiple phases depending on the couplings (μ, k₂). These phases correspond to different macroscopic geometries:

The "Crumbled" Phase — Disconnected Spacetime

For small k₂ (weak gravitational coupling), the system is in Phase A. The triangulation breaks up into disconnected clusters — spacetime itself fractures at the Planck scale. This phase is physically uninteresting as it doesn't produce a coherent 4-dimensional spacetime.

Mathematically, this corresponds to the situation where the curvature coupling is too weak to bind the simplices into a connected macroscopic geometry. The Hausdorff dimension in this phase is around 2, not 4.

The "de Sitter" Phase — Quantum Cosmology

This is the physically interesting phase! In Phase B, a macroscopic 4-dimensional spacetime emerges from the discrete triangulations. Remarkably, measurements show the effective dimension is D_H ≈ 4, and the volume-propertime relationship matches that of a de Sitter universe (like our observed cosmos).

The quantum fluctuations in this phase are relatively small, allowing a classical geometry to emerge. This is where CDT makes contact with observable cosmology.

The "Tortuous" Phase — fractal Spacetime

For large μ values, Phase C exhibits highly elongated, fractal-like structures. The effective dimension drops to D_H ≈ 2-3. These configurations resemble branched polymers or spacetime with wormholes.

This phase is avoided in the physically relevant regime as it doesn't produce smooth 4-dimensional geometry.

The Bifurcation Point — Critical Behavior

At the triple point where Phases A, B, and C meet, interesting critical behavior may occur. Some analyses suggest this could be a point of enhanced symmetry where different phases merge.

The transition between phases A and B is second-order (continuous), making it possible to probe the behavior at the critical point through finite-size scaling analysis.

Emergent Dimensionality

One of the most striking results in CDT is that the effective dimension of spacetime emerges from the dynamics, rather than being put in by hand. By measuring how the volume grows with distance in the triangulation, we can extract a "Hausdorff dimension" D_H:

How to Measure D_H

Pick a random vertex as origin. For each distance r (measured along the triangulation), count the average number of vertices within that geodesic distance. Plot V(r) vs r on log-log scales. The slope gives D_H.

Results in Phase B

In the de Sitter phase (Phase B), CDT simulations yield D_H ≈ 4 for intermediate scales, confirming that 4-dimensional geometry genuinely emerges from the discrete dynamics. This is a non-trivial result — no one put in D=4 explicitly!

Cosmological Applications: The Big Bounce

Perhaps the most dramatic prediction of CDT is what happens at the Planck scale. Unlike in classical GR where we encounter the singularity at t = 0, CDT suggests the universe undergoes a "quantum bounce". The discrete triangulations cannot collapse to zero volume — quantum effects repulsive at short distances prevent the formation of a singularity.

This is similar to what Loop Quantum Gravity (LQC) predicts, but CDT arrives at this result through a completely different mathematical framework. The bounce occurs at a critical density of order the Planck density ρ_P ≈ 5.1 × 10⁹⁷ kg/m³.

H² = (8πG/3)ρ(1 - ρ/ρ_c) LQC/CDT modified Friedmann equation preventing singularity

CDT vs LQC

CDT and Loop Quantum Cosmology arrive at similar conclusions (a bounce rather than a singularity) but through different mathematics. CDT uses path integrals over triangulations; LQC uses canonical quantization of cosmological models. Their convergence strengthens confidence in the result.

Observable Predictions

If the bounce occurred, there should be a primordial gravitational wave background with a characteristic spectrum. The quantum geometry fluctuations leave imprints. Future GW detectors (LIGO, ET, LISA) might search for this "bounce signature" in the stochastic background.

Historical Development

1990
Ambjørn and Jurkiewicz propose the first version of Dynamical Triangulations (DT) in 4 dimensions. The approach shows promise but lacks causal structure.
1998
Renate Loll, Jan Ambjørn, and Jerzy Jurkiewicz introduce the causal constraint, creating Causal Dynamical Triangulations. This seminal paper establishes the framework still used today.
2003
First numerical simulations in Phase B show effective D=4 emerging. The de Sitter phase is identified and studied with increasing statistics.
2005
The phase structure is mapped out in detail. Discovery of the bifurcation point where three phases meet. Improved algorithms allow larger volumes (N₄ ~ 10⁵).
2010
CDT group in Amsterdam and Kraków produces high-precision measurements of D_H, confirming D=4 in Phase B. Connection to LQC bounce scenarios explored.
2016
Coupling matter fields to CDT triangulations shows robustness of the D=4 result. Semi-classical limit is further constrained.
2020+
CDT continues to develop. Recent work focuses on: finite-size scaling at critical points, improved Monte Carlo algorithms, and potential observational signatures in primordial gravitational waves.
