About Gravitational Lensing

Gravitational lensing occurs when light from a distant source (like a galaxy or quasar) passes near a massive object and bends due to the curvature of spacetime predicted by Einstein's General Relativity.

Key Applications

Used to map dark matter distribution, discover exoplanets, measure Hubble constant, and probe the nature of dark energy. The effect was first confirmed during the 1919 solar eclipse expedition.

The Physics of Light Bending

Deflection Angle

When light passes near a massive object, it deflects by an angle proportional to the mass and inversely proportional to the impact parameter.

α = 4GM/(c²b)

Einstein Radius

The characteristic radius of an Einstein ring forms when source, lens, and observer are perfectly aligned.

θE = √(4GM/(c²D))

Mass Distribution Effects

Point masses produce simple lensing. Extended masses like galaxies create gradual deflection and distorted images without sharp caustics.

β > θE → Multiple images

Time Delay in Lensing

Light rays taking different paths arrive at different times. This cosmological time delay depends on the Hubble constant.

Δt = (1+z)·D/(cH₀)
