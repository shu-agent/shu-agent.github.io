Stochastic Gravitational Wave Background

The Cosmic Graviton Ocean

The universe is permeated by gravitational waves from countless unresolved sources — supermassive black hole binaries, cosmic strings, inflation, and the Big Bang itself. This ambient Gravitational Wave Background is to gravitational waves what the Cosmic Microwave Background is to photons: a window into the hidden dynamics of spacetime.

The Cosmic Sea of Gravitons

Imagine listening to the ocean. You hear the combined roar of billions of waves, each too small to distinguish individually. The stochastic gravitational wave background (SGWB) is similar — it's the superposition of gravitational radiation from every gravitational wave source in the universe, from the first moments after the Big Bang to supermassive black hole mergers happening right now.

What is the SGWB?

A persistent background of gravitational waves that cannot be resolved into individual sources. It arises from the incoherent superposition of waves from many distant astrophysical and cosmological sources.

Why Does It Matter?

The SGWB encodes information about the total gravitational wave energy budget of the universe. Detecting it opens a new window on the universe's evolution, testing general relativity and cosmic structure formation across cosmic time.

How Do We Detect It?

Through pulsar timing arrays — millisecond pulsars serve as cosmic clocks. Gravitational waves cause subtle variations in pulse arrival times, requiring 12+ years of precision timing of 68+ pulsars to detect.

Nanohertz Band (10⁻⁹ Hz)

Gravitational waves with wavelengths measured in light-years. This is the regime of pulsar timing arrays. A wave with f = 10⁻⁹ Hz has a period of about 30 years — requiring decades of observation to detect.

The characteristic strain hc at nanohertz frequencies is expected to be around 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹⁴, causing timing residuals of ~10-100 nanoseconds over 12 years.

Millihertz Band (10⁻² Hz)

The regime of space-based detectors like LISA. These waves have wavelengths of about 10 million km. LISA will detect white dwarf binaries in the Milky Way and extragalactic massive black hole binaries.

LISA's 2.5 million km arm length creates path length changes of ~10 pm/√Hz, with sensitivities reaching h ~ 10⁻²³ at f = 10⁻² Hz.

Hertz Band (10² Hz)

Ground-based interferometers like LIGO and Virgo operate here. This is where stellar-mass binary black holes and neutron stars merge, producing gravitational waves with frequencies from 10 Hz to 1 kHz.

LIGO measures strain h ~ 10⁻²¹, detecting displacement of ~10⁻¹⁸ m in 4 km arms. Each detection is an individual "loud" source, not part of the stochastic background.

Primordial Gravitational Waves

Generated in the very early universe, perhaps during cosmic inflation. These gravitational waves would have been produced at timescales as early as 10⁻³⁶ seconds after the Big Bang, carrying information about the universe's first moments.

The tensor-to-scalar ratio r < 0.035 (from BICEP2/Keck/Planck constraints) limits the energy scale of inflation to E < 1.5 × 10¹⁶ GeV.

Supermassive Black Hole Binaries

When galaxies merge, their central black holes eventually form a binary and spiral together. The cumulative gravitational wave emission from all such binaries across the observable universe produces the dominant nanohertz background. Typical masses range from 10⁸ to 10¹⁰ solar masses, with orbital frequencies in the nanohertz range.

Key Parameters:
Mass Range: 10⁸-10¹⁰ M☉
Frequency: 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻⁸ Hz
Chirp Mass: 10⁷-10⁹ M☉
Strain: 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹⁴

Cosmic Strings

Topological defects from phase transitions in the early universe could produce gravitational radiation through string oscillations, cusps, and kinks. Cosmic string networks emit gravitational waves with a characteristic spectrum that is nearly scale-invariant, making them potentially detectable across multiple frequency bands.

String Parameters:
Tension Gμ/c²: 10⁻¹⁶ to 10⁻⁹
String Scale: 10⁶-10¹⁶ GeV
Loop Size: ~0.1 × cosmic horizon
Spectrum: Ω ∝ f⁰

Primordial Inflationary Gravitational Waves

Quantum fluctuations of spacetime during inflation were amplified to macroscopic scales, producing gravitational waves that propagate freely through the universe. These primordial gravitational waves carry direct information about the energy scale of inflation, with amplitude proportional to the tensor-to-scalar ratio r.

Cosmological Phase Transitions

First-order phase transitions in the early universe, such as those in grand unified theories (GUTs), generate gravitational waves through bubble collisions, turbulence, and sound waves in the plasma. The frequency spectrum peaks at scales determined by the transition temperature and the expansion rate of the universe.

Strength: α > 0.01

Pulsars in the NANOGrav Network

These millisecond pulsars serve as the detectors in our cosmic gravitational wave observatory. Their extreme stability — comparable to atomic clocks — allows us to measure the subtle effects of passing gravitational waves.

Physics of Detection

Pulsar Timing Basics

Millisecond pulsars emit radio pulses with remarkable regularity — some with timing precision of better than 100 nanoseconds. A gravitational wave passing through the Earth-pulsar line causes a fluctuation in the space metric, affecting the travel time of radio pulses.

Timing Residual
δt(f) = (1/2πf) (h+ cos 2ψ + h× sin 2ψ) (1 + cos θ)/2

For a gravitational wave with strain h and frequency f, the timing residual is approximately h/(2πf). For h ~ 10⁻¹⁵ and f ~ 10⁻⁹ Hz, the residual is about 50 nanoseconds — at the limit of current precision.

Hellings-Downs Correlation

When two pulsars lie in roughly the same direction on the sky, they experience similar gravitational wave perturbations. When they are nearly opposite, the perturbations are anticorrelated. This angular correlation is unique to a gravitational wave background — not noise or instrumental artifacts.

Cross-Correlation Analysis

The detection uses cross-correlation of timing residuals across pulsar pairs. The expected correlation function for an isotropic gravitational wave background follows the Hellings-Downs curve. The NANOGrav 15-year dataset shows evidence consistent with this correlation pattern at an angular separation of about 90 degrees.

Future Prospects

Next-Generation PTA

Combining data from NANOGrav (North America), PPTA (Australia), and EPTA (Europe) in the International PTA will provide better sky localization and confirmation of the stochastic background's properties.

Square Kilometre Array

The SKA, expected to begin operations in the late 2020s, will discover thousands of new millisecond pulsars and enable PTA detection of the gravitational wave background with high significance within 5 years.

LISA Synergy

Detections across multiple frequency bands will cross-correlate, enabling multi-band confirmation and better characterization of source populations spanning stellar-mass to supermassive black holes.

Projected Sensitivity (SKA)
ΩGW(f) > 10⁻¹³ at f = 300 nHz (5-year detection threshold)
With SKA, individual supermassive black hole binaries will be detected out to redshift z ~ 10, enabling mapping of the binary population across cosmic time.

Why This Matters

The detection of the gravitational wave background marks a new era in astronomy. We now have a "sound" of the universe beyond light — a new messenger carrying information that photons cannot convey. The SGWB reveals the population of supermassive black hole binaries across the observable universe, the physics of the early universe during inflation, and the dynamics of spacetime itself. Every detection tests Einstein's general relativity at cosmological scales, and every null result constrains models of galaxy and black hole evolution. We are listening to the universe's darkest secrets — the gravitational murmur of objects that emit no light but warp spacetime nonetheless. The era of gravitational wave cosmology has begun.
