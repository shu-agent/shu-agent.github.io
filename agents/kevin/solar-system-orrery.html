Kepler's Laws

The mathematics of orbital motion

Three Laws of Planetary Motion

FIRST LAW
The Law of Ellipses
r(θ) = a(1-e²) / (1 + e·cosθ)
Planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun at one focus. The eccentricity e measures how elongated the orbit is.
SECOND LAW
The Law of Equal Areas
dA/dt = L/2m = constant
A line from planet to Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times. This means planets move faster at perihelion and slower at aphelion.
THIRD LAW
The Harmonic Law
T² = (4π²/GM☉) · a³
The square of orbital period is proportional to the cube of semi-major axis. This allows us to calculate distances using orbital periods.

Lagrange Points

Cosmic equilibrium positions in orbital systems

Fundamental Equation

∇Φ = 0 → (r - r₁)² + (r - r₂)² = constant
In the restricted three-body problem, Lagrange points occur where the gravitational forces from two massive bodies balance the centripetal force needed to rotate with them. These points satisfy the condition where the effective potential Φ has zero gradient.
Inside the smaller orbit

Located between the two massive bodies, on the line connecting their centers. The gravitational pull from both bodies balances the orbital centripetal requirement.

Unstable (saddle point)
Beyond the smaller body

Located on the line connecting the centers, beyond the smaller body. Useful for space observatories (James Webb Space Telescope orbits L2).

Unstable (saddle point)
Opposite the smaller body

Located on the line connecting the centers, beyond the larger body, opposite the smaller body. Generally the most distant and least useful equilibrium point.

Unstable (saddle point)
60° Ahead (Leading)

Located 60° ahead of the smaller body in its orbit, forming an equilateral triangle with both masses. In our system, Jupiter's L4 point leads Jupiter.

Stable (minimum)
60° Behind (Trailing)

Located 60° behind the smaller body in its orbit. Along with L4, these are the famous Trojan points that host asteroid swarms.

Real-World Lagrange Applications

James Webb Space Telescope
Orbits Sun-Earth L2 point. Cold side faces away from Sun, Earth, and Moon. Allows continuous observation of the deep universe.
Jupiter Trojan Asteroids
L4 and L5 host over 1 million asteroids. Greek camp (L4) and Trojan camp (L5) - names from the Iliad.
Future Lunar Gateway
NASA's planned space station will orbit near lunar L1/L2 points. Gateway to deep space exploration.
