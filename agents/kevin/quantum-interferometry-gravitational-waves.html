Fundamentals of Optical Interferometry

Michelson Interferometer Principle

The Michelson interferometer, invented in 1887 by Albert Michelson, splits a single beam of light into two perpendicular paths using a semi-transparent mirror (beam splitter). After reflecting off end mirrors, the beams recombine to produce interference patterns.

  • Path difference creates constructive or destructive interference
  • Fringe shifts indicate changes in optical path length
  • Fundamental to all modern gravitational wave detectors
  • Sensitivity limited by photon counting statistics

Interference Conditions

Constructive interference occurs when the optical path difference equals an integer multiple of the wavelength (mλ), while destructive interference happens at half-integer multiples ((m+½)λ).

ΔL = mλ (constructive) | ΔL = (m + ½)λ (destructive)
Where ΔL is path difference, m is integer, λ is wavelength

Coherence Length

The coherence length Lc determines the maximum path difference for observable interference. For laser light, coherence lengths can exceed kilometers, essential for kilometer-scale gravitational wave detectors.

L_c = c·τ_c = λ²/Δλ
Coherence time τc relates to spectral width

Finesse Factor

The finesse F characterizes an interferometer's resolution relative to its free spectral range. Fabry-Perot cavities in LIGO achieve finesse values of ~450, dramatically enhancing effective path length.

F = π·√r₁r₂ / (1 - r₁r₂)
r₁, r₂ are mirror reflectivity coefficients

Shot Noise Limit

Quantum photon statistics impose a fundamental noise floor. The minimum detectable strain scales as the inverse square root of optical power and finesse.

h_shot = 1/(L·F·√P) · √(hc/λ)
L = arm length, P = laser power

LIGO & Kilometer-Scale Interferometry

LIGO Configuration

The Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory uses a modified Michelson interferometer with Fabry-Perot cavities in each arm. The 4km arm length is enhanced by ~270 round trips through each cavity, achieving an effective path length of ~1000km.

  • Input laser: 1064nm Nd:YAG, 200W output
  • Fabry-Perot cavity finesse: ~450
  • Arm cavity enhanced path: ~1000 km effective
  • Recycling mirror increases effective power

Power Recycling Technique

By placing a partially reflecting "recycling mirror" at the input, LIGO increases the effective optical power circulating in the interferometer by a factor of ~40, significantly improving sensitivity beyond shot noise limits.

  • Input power: 200W laser → 7 kW circulating
  • Signal recycling adds additional control
  • Enhanced strain sensitivity by factor ~40
  • Enables quantum noise reduction techniques

LIGO Sensitivity Curve

The characteristic "bucket" shape shows strain sensitivity as a function of frequency. At low frequencies (< 10 Hz), seismic noise dominates. Around 100 Hz, quantum radiation pressure noise becomes limiting. The peak sensitivity near 100-200 Hz enables detection of binary black hole mergers.

LIGO Noise Budget Breakdown

Quantum Noise Sources

  • Shot Noise: h_shot ~ √(ħω)/(2LP) — photon counting statistics limit high-frequency sensitivity
  • Radiation Pressure: h_rp ~ (2FLP)/(c³ω) × √(ħω) — photon momentum imparts force to mirrors
  • Squeezed Vacuum: Reduces quantum noise by factor e^(-r) in one quadrature

Classical Noise Sources

  • Seismic Noise: Requires multi-stage isolation platforms, performance ~ (10Hz/f)²
  • Thermal Noise: Substrate and coating noise, ~ 10^-24 strain/√Hz at 100Hz
  • Gas Density: Residual gas molecules scatter light, requires ultra-high vacuum
h_total = √(h_shot² + h_rp² + h_seismic² + h_thermal² + ...)
Total noise is root-sum-square of independent contributors

Advanced LIGO+ Improvements

Advanced LIGO+ implements several key upgrades to improve sensitivity beyond initial LIGO:

  • Quantum Enhancement: 10 dB frequency-dependent squeezing injected at 10-200 Hz bands
  • Output Mode Cleaner: Filters spurious higher-order optical modes
  • Mirror Coating: Improved SiO₂/Ta₂O₅ dielectric coatings reduce thermal noise by 2x
  • Input Power: Increased to 35W laser input, raising circulating power to ~700 kW
  • Signal Recycling: Tunable signal recycling mirror for narrowband operation
h_min = h_SQL · e^(-r) / √(1 + r²sin²(2φ))
Squeezed state sensitivity with optimal angle φ and squeeze parameter r

Quantum Noise & Squeezed Light

The Standard Quantum Limit

In a simple interferometer, Heisenberg's uncertainty principle imposes a fundamental limit on simultaneous measurement precision of amplitude and phase. The SQL represents the optimal sensitivity achievable with coherent laser light.

h_SQL = √(ħ) / (L·m·ω²)
m = mirror mass, ω = measurement frequency

Quantum Squeezing

Squeezed states redistribute quantum uncertainty from one quadrature (e.g., phase) to the conjugate (e.g., amplitude). This "squeezes" noise below the SQL in the phase measurement direction.

  • Inject squeezed vacuum via readout port
  • Frequency-dependent squeezing for broad-band improvement
  • 10 dB squeezing achieved in LIGO (2024)
  • Future: 20 dB squeezing planned for ET

Two-Photon Modulation Formalism

Quantum optics of interferometers is elegantly described by two-photon formalism. The output field's quadrature operators X and Y evolve with respect to the input squeezed quadratures.

X_out = cos(r)·X_in + sin(r)·Y_in
Output quadrature as rotation in phase space

Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen Correlations

By exploiting quantum entanglement between light and mirrors, future "entangler" schemes may achieve sensitivities below standard quantum limits through EPR correlations.

  • Bell inequality tests with macroscopic mirrors
  • Quantum non-demolition measurements
  • Backaction-evading measurements

Advanced Interferometer Configurations

Sagnac Interferometer

Light travels both clockwise and counterclockwise in a rotating interferometer. The Sagnac effect creates a phase shift proportional to rotation rate Ω. Used in gyroscopes and theoretical gravitational wave detection.

Δφ = (4π·A·Ω) / (λ·c)
A = enclosed area, Ω = rotation rate

Fabry-Perot Cavity

Two parallel mirrors form an optical resonator. Resonant enhancement multiplies effective path length by factor 2F/π. Essential for LIGO's kilometer-scale effective arm length.

P_circ = P_in · (1 + r)/(1 - r) · (1 + cos(φ))
Resonant enhancement for finesse F ~ π/(1-r)

Michelson-Sagnac Hybrid

Ring interferometers can detect gravitational waves through Sagnac effect. The response depends on interferometer topology. Sagnac configuration is insensitive to common-mode noise.

h(ω) = (ΔL/L) · sin(ω·τ) / (ω·τ)
Frequency response of Sagnac GW detector

Dual Recycled Interferometer

Modern gravitational wave detectors use both power recycling (PRM) and signal recycling (SRM). This "dual recycled" configuration provides independent control over sensitivity at different frequencies.

  • PRM increases circulating power by factor ~40
  • SRM allows narrowband operation at specific frequencies
  • Tunable detuning enables targeted source searches
  • Enables frequency-dependent squeezing injection

Detuned Signal Recycling

When the signal recycling mirror is detuned from resonance, the interferometer becomes sensitive to gravitational waves in a narrow frequency band. This enhances signal-to-noise ratio for anticipated sources.

f_spring = (c / 4πL) · arccos(g_s)
Signal recycling creates optical spring at frequency f_spring

The "optical spring" effect can lock the interferometer to a gravitational wave signal, enabling coherent tracking.

Broadband Configuration

For widest possible sensitivity, signal recycling is tuned to resonance (Broadband Mode). This sacrifices peak sensitivity for uniform coverage across the detection band.

Quantum Measurement Theory in Interferometry

Quantum Nondemolition (QND) Measurements

QND principles enable measurement of a quantum variable without disturbing it beyond the standard quantum limit. For gravitational wave detection, this means measuring phase without adding excess noise.

  • Phase estimation beyond SQL using weak measurements
  • Backaction evasion through conditional preparation
  • Quantum feedback for real-time noise suppression

Bayesian Phase Estimation

Modern quantum metrology uses Bayesian inference to extract gravitational wave signals. The posterior distribution of phase encodes both signal and noise information optimally.

P(φ|D) ∝ P(D|φ)·P(φ)
Bayes theorem for phase estimation from data D

Heisenberg Uncertainty & the SQL

The standard quantum limit arises from the commutator [X, P] = iħ. Simultaneous measurement of non-commuting observables introduces additional noise proportional to the measurement strength.

ΔX·ΔP ≥ ħ/2 (Heisenberg)
Fundamental limit on conjugate variable precision
Δφ_SQL = 1 / √N (Standard QL)
Phase precision with N photons

Quantum Metrology Advantage

Using entangled states (NOON states, twin beams), quantum metrology can surpass the SQL with Heisenberg scaling: Δφ ∝ 1/N, potentially revolutionary for future detectors.

  • NOON states: Δφ ∝ 1/N with N photons
  • Twin-beam entanglement: 3 dB quantum advantage
  • Gaussian entanglement: modest but practical gains
  • Current LIGO squeezing: ~3 dB effective gain

Historical Milestones

1887
Michelson-Morley Experiment
Albert Michelson and Edward Morley attempt to detect the luminiferous aether using an optical interferometer, establishing the foundation for relativity.
1893
Michelson Interferometer Patented
Michelson patents his interferometer design, recognizing its potential for precision measurement of length standards.
1968
Weber's Resonant Bar Detectors
Joseph Weber builds the first gravitational wave detectors using aluminum bars, pioneering the search for astrophysical gravitational waves.
1972
Forward's Interferometer Design
Rainer Forward proposes the first practical laser interferometer design for gravitational wave detection, ancestor of modern LIGO.
1980
LIGO Conceptual Design
Rainer Weiss, Kip Thorne, and Ronald Drever develop the conceptual design for a kilometer-scale laser interferometer gravitational wave detector.
1991
GEO600 Construction Begins
German-British collaboration begins construction of GEO600, a 600m interferometer in Germany, developing key technologies for LIGO.
1999
LIGO First Light
Initial LIGO achieves first light, beginning operations with 4km arms and initial sensitivity targeting binary neutron star mergers.
2015
First Direct Detection
Advanced LIGO detects gravitational wave signal GW150914 from binary black hole merger at 1.3 billion light-years, confirming Einstein's prediction.
2017
Neutron Star Merger GW170817
LIGO-Virgo detects binary neutron star merger, followed by kilonova electromagnetic counterpart observed by 70 telescopes worldwide.
2020
Quantum Enhancement Achieved
Advanced LIGO+ implements 10 dB quantum squeezing, becoming the first astronomical instrument to exceed the standard quantum limit.
2024
Multi-Detector Network
LIGO (Hanford, Livingston, India), Virgo, KAGRA operate as global network, enabling first triangulation of gravitational wave sources.
2030s
Einstein Telescope & LCE
Next-generation ground-based detectors (10-40km scale) and LISA space interferometer will extend detection tomilliHertz frequencies, observing supermassive black holes.

The Quantum Horizon of Gravitational Wave Science

From Michelson's original interferometer to today's quantum-enhanced detectors, the quest to measure gravitational waves has pushed the boundaries of both physics and engineering. As we enter the era of quantum-limited precision measurement, new horizons emerge—entangled states, quantum non-demolition measurements, and space-based interferometry promise to reveal gravitational wave signals from sources yet unseen, from the infant universe to the mergers of supermassive black holes.
