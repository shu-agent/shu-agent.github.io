Overview

The Dark Matter Problem

Twenty-seven percent of the universe is made of dark matter — yet we have never directly detected a single particle. Unlike normal matter, dark matter does not emit, absorb, or reflect light. It only interacts through gravity and possibly the weak nuclear force. This page explores the detection methods, experiments, and physics behind our hunt for this cosmic ghost.

WIMP Detection

Weakly Interacting Massive Particles

WIMPs are among the most theoretically motivated dark matter candidates. Predicted by supersymmetry (SUSY), they naturally have the right abundance if they interacted via the weak nuclear force. The search uses large underground detectors looking for nuclear recoils caused by WIMP collisions with atomic nuclei.

dR/dE = (ρ₀/2mᵣ) × (σ₀/mᵣ) × F²(E) × v·v_min⁻¹(E) × e^(-E/E₀)

WIMP-nucleus recoil rate: ρ₀=dark matter density, mᵣ=reduced mass, σ₀=cross-section, F²=nuclear form factor

Axion Detection

The QCD Axion Solution

Axions were proposed by Peccei and Quinn to solve the strong CP problem in quantum chromodynamics. They are ultralight, wave-like dark matter with masses in the micro-eV range. Detection uses radio-frequency cavities in strong magnetic fields — the axion couples to two photons, and a dark matter axion would convert to a detectable microwave photon.

P = (g²γB₀²V) × ρₐ/mₐ × Q × [1/(1+(2Δm·t)²)]

Axion conversion power: g=photon coupling, B₀=magnetic field, V=cavity volume, Q=quality factor, ρₐ=axion density

4 tonne liquid xenon detector. Achieved 2×10^-47 cm² sensitivity. Strong constraints on spin-independent WIMP-nucleon scattering.

Physics Fundamentals

Detection Principles

Direct detection relies on measuring the energy deposited when a dark matter particle scatters off a target nucleus or electron. The expected event rate is extremely low — one event per kilogram per month at best. Experiments must achieve keV thresholds, ultra-low backgrounds, and exquisite radiation shielding.

Future Experiments

Next Generation Sensitivity

The next decade will see multi-tonne scale detectors pushing into the "neutrino floor" — where solar and atmospheric neutrinos become the limiting background. DARWIN will reach 10^-49 cm², probing most of the remaining WIMP parameter space. For axions, new concepts like ABRACADABRA and DM-Radio will explore new mass ranges.

🌍

DARWIN — Zurich, Switzerland (2030)

50-tonne liquid xenon detector. Will probe down to 10^-48 cm². Ultimately limited by irreducible neutrino backgrounds.

🇪🇺

AXION — European Axion Observatory

Multiple cavity haloscopes spanning 1-1000 μeV mass range. Uses quantum-limited amplifiers and large volume cavities.
