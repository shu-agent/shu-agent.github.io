Volume I: Foundations of Quantum Field Theory

The foundational text establishing the mathematical framework of QFT, from canonical quantization to the LSZ reduction formula. Weinberg builds the theory with remarkable pedagogical clarity, starting from the simplest systems and progressively introducing complexity.

Chapter 1-2
Historical Origins & Relativistic Quantum Mechanics
Begins with the failures of single-particle quantum mechanics and introduces the need for field quantization. Dirac's groundbreaking 1927 paper on the quantum theory of the radiation field sets the stage.
i∂ψ/∂t = Hψ (fails for relativistic particles)
Chapter 3-4
Canonical Quantization & Path Integrals
Two complementary approaches to field quantization. The path integral formulation, developed by Feynman, provides intuitive understanding of quantum dynamics.
Z = ∫ Dφ exp(iS[φ]/ℏ)
Chapter 5-6
Lorentz Invariance & Discrete Symmetries
The power of symmetry principles in constructing field theories. P, C, and CP violation revealed through elegant group theoretical methods.
Chapter 7-8
Free Fields & Propagators
Quantization of scalar, spinor, and vector fields. The propagator formalism enables calculation of transition amplitudes.
D(x-y) = ⟨0|Tφ(x)φ(y)|0⟩
Chapter 9-10
Interactions & Perturbation Theory
Wicks theorem and the LSZ reduction formula connect field theory to observable scattering amplitudes. Feynman diagrams provide the computational tool.
Chapter 11-12
Cross Sections & Decay Rates
How to calculate physical observables from S-matrix elements. The optical theorem and unitarity constraints.
dσ = |M|² dΦ₂/(2E₁2E₂|v₁-v₂|)

Volume II: Modern Applications

A comprehensive treatment of gauge theories and the Standard Model. Weinberg masterfully develops the electroweak theory and QCD, showing how symmetry principles and renormalization yield predictive power.

Gauge Theories & Noether's Theorem
Local gauge invariance mandates the existence of gauge bosons. The Standard Model emerges from requiring invariance under SU(3)×SU(2)×U(1) transformations.
∂_μ A^μ - ig[A_ν, A^ν] = J_μ
Each continuous symmetry gives rise to a conserved current via Noether's theorem. Gauge symmetries are not symmetries but redundancy in description.
Electroweak Synthesis
Glashow, Weinberg, and Salam showed how the electromagnetic and weak forces unify at energies above ~100 GeV. The Higgs mechanism provides mass to W and Z bosons while keeping the photon massless.
M_W = M_Z cosθ_W = (gv/2)
The Higgs vacuum expectation value v ≈ 246 GeV sets the electroweake scale.
Quantum Chromodynamics
The theory of strong interactions describes quarks and gluons. Asymptotic freedom means quarks behave as free particles at high energies, enabling perturbative calculations. Confinement ensures quarks never appear alone.
α_s(Q²) = α_s(M²) / (1 + (α_s/12π)ln(Q²/M²))
Renormalization Group
Wilson's revolutionary insight: infinities in QFT arise from our ignorance of short-distance physics. The renormalization group tracks how couplings run with energy scale.
β(g) = μ∂g/∂μ = -b₀g³/(16π²) + O(g⁵)
Critical phenomena near phase transitions exhibit universal scaling laws determined by renormalization group fixed points.

Volume III: Supersymmetry & Beyond

The frontier of QFT: supersymmetry extends spacetime symmetry to include fermionic generators. This volume covers supergravity, extended objects, and provides foundations for string theory.

Supercharge Q Boson Fermion Spin Change
Q|boson⟩ = |fermion⟩ Photon (γ) Photino (γ̃) s: 1 → 1/2
Q|fermion⟩ = |boson⟩ Gluon (g) Gluino (g̃) s: 1 → 1/2
Q|fermion⟩ = |boson⟩ W Boson (W) Wino (W̃) s: 1 → 1/2
Q|boson⟩ = |fermion⟩ Higgs (H) Higgsino (H̃) s: 0 → 1/2
Q|fermion⟩ = |boson⟩ Graviton (G) Gravitino (G̃) s: 2 → 3/2
Supergravity & Local Supersymmetry
Supergravity extends GR by including the gravitino - a spin-3/2 partner to the graviton. The theory emerges when global supersymmetry is promoted to a local symmetry.
{Q_A, Q̄_Ḃ} = 2γ^μ_AB P_μ = (2κ²) T_AB
N=8 supergravity unifies all particles in a single multiplet with 256 degrees of freedom.
Extended Objects & Branes
Point particles generalize to 1-dimensional strings, 2-dimensional membranes, and higher-dimensional branes. String theory eliminates infinities by replacing point interactions with extended objects.
S = -T ∫ d²σ √h h^{ab} ∂_a X^μ ∂_b X^ν g_μν
The string length scale ℓ_s = √(α') ≈ 10⁻³⁵ m makes direct observation impossible with current technology.

Key Insights from Weinberg's Approach

The Cluster Decomposition Principle
Physical predictions must be independent of distant experiments. This principle severely constrains theory construction and underlies the S-matrix approach.
Effective Field Theory Philosophy
QFT is not just about fundamental constituents but about effective descriptions valid up to some energy scale. Weinberg showed this is not a limitation but a feature.
L = L_pertinent + Σ(c_n/Λ^{n-4})) O_n
Renormalization as Unity Principle
The need to subtract infinities reflects the deep connection between short-distance physics and macroscopic observables. All successful QFTs are renormalizable within their domain.
Spin-Statistics Connection
Weinberg's derivation of the spin-statistics theorem from basic principles is definitive. Integer spin particles must be bosons; half-integer must be fermions.
The Power of Analyticity
Dispersion relations and analytic continuation in complex angular momentum (Regge theory) connect scattering amplitudes to bound states and resonances.
Spontaneous Symmetry Breaking
Hidden symmetry explains mass generation without violating gauge invariance. The Goldstone theorem and Higgs mechanism are elegantly unified in Weinberg's presentation.
